What Is AI Video Generation and How Does It Work?
AI video generation takes a text prompt, an image, or both, and produces a short video clip. You describe what you want, the subject, action, setting, lighting, and camera angle, and the model generates it frame by frame.
There are two workflows. Text-to-video means you write a prompt and get a video. Image-to-video means you upload a photo and the model animates it. Many beginners start with image-to-video because the output is often more predictable: the model starts from a real picture instead of interpreting everything from text alone.
The model generates the whole clip in one pass, so a single clip usually holds together better than two separate clips. Still, faces, hands, and objects can drift or change mid-clip. The bigger challenge starts when you need a second clip. Each new generation starts from scratch. Most beginners run into this after their first few clips.
What Do You Need to Get Started?
You need three things: a clear idea of the shot you want, a prompt that describes it, and a source image if you are doing image-to-video. What goes into the prompt depends on the mode.
Text-to-video:
describe the scene and the motion. The model has nothing to look at, so the prompt has to cover everything:
Subject: who or what is in the frame
Action: what they are doing
Setting: where it is happening
Camera: how the shot is framed and how it moves
Weak: “A woman walking.” Stronger: “A woman in a red coat walks through a rainy Tokyo street at night, close-up, slow motion, neon reflections on wet pavement.” The more specific you are, the less the model guesses.
Image-to-video:
describe the motion first. The image already sets the subject, setting, and look, so the prompt mostly needs to say what moves, how it moves, and what the camera does. Repeating what’s already visible in the image adds little.
A good source image for image-to-video:
Sharp focus and clear lighting
The subject that matters is clearly visible; if it’s a person, the face isn’t hidden
No heavy clutter obscuring what matters
Resolution at least as high as the video you plan to make; check the model’s upload limits
Side angles, dark lighting, and busy backgrounds often make results less predictable. You can still use them, but simpler shots are easier to start with. Don’t have a photo that fits? You can generate a source image instead, for example in

How to Generate Your First AI Video: Step by Step
Below are the six steps, with one real example made on Higgsfield: a still frame created in Soul Cinema turned into a 10-second, three-shot clip with Seedance 2.5.
Step 1. Pick the right workflow.
Image-to-video for realistic people, product demos, or any shot where you want a specific face or object. Text-to-video for abstract scenes, landscapes, motion experiments, or anything where you do not have a reference image. Open Video → Create Video and upload the image.
In our example: the reference image is a still frame we generated in Soul Cinema: a rider and a passenger on a red motorcycle with a sidecar under an elevated train line.
Step 2. Write your prompt like a director.
For text-to-video, describe the subject, action, setting, lighting, and camera in one or two sentences. For image-to-video, focus on the motion of the subject and the camera. Add a mood or style if it matters: cinematic, documentary, dreamlike. Avoid adjectives like “beautiful” or “amazing”; they give the model nothing to work with.
In our example: Our full prompt has three shots, the camera path, the light, and the sound second by second. For your first clip, two sentences about the motion are enough, for example: “The rider pulls the goggles down and starts the engine. The camera slowly pushes in as the passenger smiles at him.”

Step 5. Review the full clip.
Check whether the motion holds throughout. Check whether expressions or movements still feel natural near the end of the clip, not just at the start. Many quality problems appear mid-clip, not at the opening frame. If the clip works, download it from your generation history.
Step 6. Change one thing per iteration.
If something is off, change the prompt, the source image, or the model, one at a time. Changing all three at once makes it impossible to know what fixed it.
In our example: in the short kick-start insert, the rider’s foot rests on the footpeg instead of stamping the kick-start lever. One change to try: describe that insert in its own short paragraph so the instruction doesn’t get lost in a long prompt. A retry with the same settings costs another 160 credits.

What Are the Limits of AI Video in 2026?
Clip length.
Clip length depends on the model: up to 8 seconds on Veo 3.1, 15 on Kling 3.0, and 30 on Kling 4.0. Longer content still means chaining clips together and cutting between them in post.
Character consistency across clips.
Each new generation starts from scratch. The same face in clip 1 will look slightly different in clip 2 unless you use an identity tool like Soul ID or reference images.
Precise motion control.
Describing complex movement in text is still imprecise. “She reaches slowly toward the camera” will get interpreted differently each time. First-and-last-frame control, available in models like Wan 2.7, is the most direct solution, but it requires planning both endpoints upfront.
Multi-person scenes.
Two people interacting in the same shot, especially in close-up, often produce visible inconsistencies. Generate each person separately and composite in post if precision matters.
